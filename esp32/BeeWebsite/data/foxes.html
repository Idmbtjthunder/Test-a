The Wonderful World of Foxes

Species, facts, curiosities, and a few famous ones.

Species

Red Fox

Vulpes vulpes

The most widespread wild carnivore on Earth, found across nearly the whole Northern Hemisphere and introduced to Australia. Highly adaptable — as at home in cities as in forests and tundra.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Arctic Fox

Vulpes lagopus

Built for the cold — thick insulating fur, small ears to reduce heat loss, and a coat that turns white in winter and brown-gray in summer for year-round camouflage.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Fennec Fox

Vulpes zerda

The smallest fox species, native to the Sahara. Its enormous ears — the largest of any canid relative to body size — radiate heat and pick up prey moving underground.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Gray Fox

Urocyon cinereoargenteus

Not actually in the genus Vulpes — it belongs to the oldest living branch of the fox family. Uniquely among canids, it can climb trees using hooked claws, and will den in hollow branches.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Kit Fox

Vulpes macrotis

One of the smallest North American foxes, suited to arid deserts and grasslands. Large ears help dissipate heat, and it can go long stretches without drinking free water.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Swift Fox

Vulpes velox

A prairie specialist of the North American Great Plains. Its population crashed alongside prairie dog eradication and predator control programs, but reintroduction efforts have helped it recover in parts of its range.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Corsac Fox

Vulpes corsac

A fox of the Central Asian steppes and semi-deserts. It's more social than most foxes, sometimes seen in small groups, and migrates seasonally when prey and weather demand it.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Tibetan Sand Fox

Vulpes ferrilata

Instantly recognizable for its almost square-shaped head and narrow, close-set eyes. It lives on the high Tibetan Plateau, hunting pikas at altitudes up to 5,300 meters.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Bat-Eared Fox

Otocyon megalotis

A fox in name only — its own genus, and one of the few canids that's mostly insectivorous. It eats mostly harvester termites, using its huge ears to pinpoint insects moving underground.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Bengal Fox

Vulpes bengalensis

Endemic to the Indian subcontinent, favoring open grassland and scrub. Pairs mate for life and dig elaborate multi-entrance dens, sometimes used for generations.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Pale Fox

Vulpes pallida

A little-studied fox of the semi-arid Sahel belt, south of the Sahara. Sandy coat provides camouflage in its scrubland habitat; it remains one of the least-researched fox species.

Illustration: Wikimedia Commons

Rüppell's Fox

Vulpes rueppellii

A desert fox of North Africa and the Middle East, named after 19th-century naturalist Eduard Rüppell. Furred paw pads give it grip and insulation on hot, shifting sand.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Blanford's Fox

Vulpes cana

A small, agile fox of rocky mountain terrain across the Middle East and Central Asia. Semi-retractable claws and cat-like agility let it scramble across cliffs most predators avoid.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Culpeo (Andean Fox)

Lycalopex culpaeus

South America's second-largest native canid, and another "fox" that isn't a true fox — it's actually more closely related to wolves and jackals than to Vulpes foxes.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Facts & Curiosities

There are 12 species of "true fox" in the genus Vulpes — but several animals commonly called foxes, like the gray fox, bat-eared fox, and Andean fox, belong to entirely different genera.
A fox's bushy tail is called a "brush." It aids balance while running and jumping, signals mood and intent to other foxes, and wraps around the body like a blanket for warmth in cold weather.
Foxes have vertically slit pupils, like cats — an adaptation shared by ambush predators that hunt at dawn and dusk, helping judge distance precisely in low light.
Red foxes appear to use Earth's magnetic field when hunting. Studies suggest they line up pounces on prey hidden under snow using the angle between magnetic north and the sound they hear — a sense shared by very few animals.
The eerie "fox scream" heard at night is usually a vixen's contact or mating call. It's so unsettling that it's one of the most common sounds mistaken for a human scream or crying child.
A group of foxes is called a "skulk" or a "leash," but the name rarely applies — foxes are mostly solitary, coming together chiefly to mate and raise kits.
Foxes are omnivores, not strict carnivores. Alongside rodents and birds, they happily eat insects, earthworms, berries, and fruit depending on season and availability.
Starting in 1959, Soviet geneticist Dmitri Belyaev bred silver foxes purely for tameness. Within a few generations, the tame foxes spontaneously developed floppy ears, curled tails, and patchy coats — a landmark study in how domestication reshapes animals.
Urban foxes have thrived in cities worldwide, especially in the UK, denning under garden sheds and decking and scavenging food waste alongside — and sometimes befriending — the humans they share streets with.
A fox's whiskers aren't just on its face — they also grow on the backs of its front legs, helping it navigate and judge gaps in the dark or in dense undergrowth.

Famous Foxes

Folklore

Reynard the Fox

The cunning trickster of medieval European folk tales, forever outwitting bigger, stronger animals through wit rather than force — the archetype behind almost every clever fox since.

Folklore

Kitsune

Fox spirits in Japanese folklore, said to grow additional tails — up to nine — as they age and gain wisdom and magical power, including shapeshifting into human form.

Folklore

Aesop's Fox

The recurring sly fox of Aesop's Fables — from "The Fox and the Grapes" to "The Fox and the Crow" — cemented the fox's reputation for cunning over two thousand years ago.

Fiction

Nick Wilde

The streetwise, reformed con-artist fox from Disney's Zootopia (2016), who partners with a rabbit cop to solve a city-wide mystery.

Fiction

Tails (Miles Prower)

Sonic the Hedgehog's twin-tailed fox sidekick, able to fly by spinning his two tails like a helicopter rotor.

Fiction

Ninetales & Vulpix

Fox-inspired Pokémon directly modeled on kitsune folklore — Vulpix's six tails split into nine as it evolves into Ninetales.

Fiction

Fantastic Mr Fox

The dapper, chicken-thieving hero of Roald Dahl's 1970 novel and Wes Anderson's 2009 stop-motion film, forever one step ahead of three vengeful farmers.

Fiction

Robin Hood

Disney's 1973 animated Robin Hood reimagines the legendary outlaw as a fox — quick, charming, and always one step ahead of the Sheriff of Nottingham.

Fiction

Basil Brush

The boisterous, "boom boom!"-catchphrase fox puppet who has hosted British children's television since the late 1960s.

Fiction

General Fox McCloud

The ace pilot fox who leads the Star Fox team through Nintendo's long-running space-combat series, flying the Arwing since 1993.

Fiction

Swiper

The scheming, "Swiper, no swiping!" fox from Dora the Explorer, forever trying to snatch something that isn't his.

Real

Belyaev's Silver Foxes

The founding population of the Russian domesticated fox experiment — the most famous individual foxes in science, still studied today as a model for how domestication happens.
